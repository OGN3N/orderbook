# Comparative Evaluation, Challenges and Conclusion

## 9.1 Comparative Evaluation of Order Book Designs

The experiments do not identify one universally superior order-book representation. Instead, they show that latency is determined by the interaction between the data layout, the operation being executed, the distribution of prices, and the current state of the book. The same property can be an advantage on one path and a disadvantage on another. A fixed price array provides constant-time access to a known level, for example, but it may still require a long scan to discover which level should be accessed. An ordered tree avoids that scan, but pays for tree traversal, node management, and less contiguous storage on insertions and cancellations.

The strongest separation appears in best-price discovery. Across the uniform, clustered, Zipfian, bursty, and high-cancellation workloads, the fixed-grid implementations repeatedly scanned large empty regions before reaching active asks or bids. Depending on the scenario and percentile, their market-order latency was tens of times greater than that of the hybrid or B-tree implementations. This result is not a general rejection of arrays. It shows that direct indexing solves access by a *known* price, whereas a market order first requires ordered discovery of an *unknown* executable price. When the occupied range is sparse and far from the array boundary at which the search begins, discovery dominates the cost of matching.

Insertion generally produces the opposite ranking. Fixed-tick AoS has the lowest latency in the uniform workload and remains stable as the number of resting orders increases. Under clustered, Zipfian, and bursty prices, fixed-tick AoS, fixed-tick SoA, and Hybrid tie at the median because their active paths use direct indexing and repeatedly access a small price region. Hybrid is strongest in the high-cancellation and stable-depth workloads, in which all prices deliberately remain inside its hot array. The B-tree is usually slower on this path because every insertion must locate or create an ordered-map entry. These results support fixed indexing when the valid price domain is bounded and mutation latency is more important than sparse ordered traversal.

Cancellation depends on two distinct lookups: finding the order's price through the identifier index and finding its position within the FIFO queue at that price. Since all four implementations use an auxiliary identifier map, differences are smaller than for market orders. SoA provides a limited benefit when price clustering creates deep queues: its compact identifier vector produces the lowest p99 cancellation latency in the clustered scenario and improves on fixed-tick AoS in the Zipfian tail. However, after finding an identifier, SoA must remove aligned entries from several parallel vectors. That additional maintenance prevents it from becoming consistently fastest and increases the implementation burden because the field arrays must never become unsynchronized.

The sweep experiment demonstrates that the preferred representation can change within a single operation type. Hybrid is fastest for 5-, 20-, and 50-level sweeps contained in its configured hot region. At 150 levels, the sweep crosses into the cold tree and Hybrid becomes the slowest implementation, while fixed-tick AoS becomes the fastest. Once a fixed-grid sweep has paid its initial discovery cost, consecutive occupied levels are cheap to traverse and the fixed cost is amortized over a larger match. The B-tree scales more consistently across sweep depths, without either the initial long array scan or the Hybrid boundary transition. The relevant workload property is therefore not only the typical location of resting orders, but also the distance an aggressive order is likely to travel through the book.

The build-up experiment adds a lifecycle perspective. Median insertion latency does not increase monotonically with total book depth, and fixed-tick AoS remains stable across every sampled window. All implementations have a heavier p99 during the first 500 additions to a newly constructed book. The current measurements cannot attribute this effect to a single cause, but allocation and growth of auxiliary structures are plausible contributors. Capacity planning, preallocation, and warm-up behavior consequently deserve attention even if mature-book median latency is the primary target.

The comparative position of each design can be summarized as follows.

| Design | Observed strengths | Principal costs and risks | Most suitable conditions indicated by this study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed-tick AoS | Fast direct insertion and cancellation; simple price-to-index mapping; stable insertion median; efficient long traversal of consecutive levels | Memory is reserved for the complete price grid; sparse best-price discovery scans empty levels; price domain must be bounded | Bounded, sufficiently dense price spaces; mutation-heavy traffic; deep sweeps over consecutive levels |
| Fixed-tick SoA | Compact field-specific scans; lower cancellation tail than AoS in some deep-queue cases; cheaper empty-level scanning than AoS in some scenarios | Multiple vectors must be updated consistently; matching and insertion touch several arrays; does not solve empty-price discovery | Workloads that repeatedly inspect one field within deep price-level queues |
| B-tree AoS | Efficient best-price discovery in sparse books; naturally ordered and dynamic price set; consistent behavior across locality changes and sweep depths | Tree lookup, allocation, pointer chasing, and node-management overhead on mutations | Sparse or wide price distributions; unpredictable active ranges; market-order-sensitive workloads |
| Hybrid | Combines fast access near the reference price with ordered cold storage; strongest overall results when traffic remains in the hot zone; fast ordinary sweeps | Performance depends on window placement and width; hot/cold transitions add complexity and can dominate cross-boundary sweeps | Instruments with stable, measurable locality around a reference price and mostly local aggressive orders |

The two isolated memory experiments reinforce the same workload-dependent interpretation. Reducing an order record from 24 to 17 bytes improves the median sequential-read batch by 25% without materially changing vector-construction latency, while expanding it to 64 bytes makes sequential traversal and construction substantially slower. This supports minimizing the bytes transferred by read-heavy paths, although the packed representation must still be evaluated inside a complete order book before an end-to-end benefit can be claimed. Likewise, none of the tested software-prefetch strategies improves latency. Their address calculations, conditions, and hint instructions cost more than the latency they attempt to hide under the tested cache-resident and sparse-access conditions. Low-level optimizations should therefore follow measurement of the actual bottleneck rather than be assumed beneficial from their intended hardware effect.

Taken together, the experiments favor an operation-aware design process. The relevant questions are which fields an operation reads, whether its target price is already known, how many empty levels it may cross, how deep queues become at popular prices, and whether an access remains inside a stable locality window. Big-O descriptions remain useful, but they are not sufficient to predict matching-engine latency because constant factors, bytes touched, allocation, and transition costs are visible at this timescale.

## 9.2 Challenges and Future Directions

Several limitations constrain the scope of the conclusions. First, the benchmarks use deterministic synthetic workloads. Uniform, clustered, Zipfian, and bursty models isolate important properties, but they do not reproduce the complete dependence structure of a live event stream. Real markets contain intraday regime changes, correlated submissions and cancellations, price moves, replace operations, partial fills, and instrument-specific tick and depth characteristics. The reported rankings are therefore evidence for the tested access patterns, not universal claims about all exchanges or instruments.

Second, the evaluation measures single-threaded operation latency on one x86-64 system. It does not measure throughput, performance under contention, inter-core communication, NUMA effects, or the integration costs of risk checks, market-data publication, journaling, and recovery. Production matching engines commonly partition work by instrument, but concurrency remains relevant at ingress, egress, and shared services. A representation that is best in an isolated book may behave differently once synchronization, ownership transfer, persistence, and backpressure are included.

Third, the measurement environment limits causal attribution. The benchmark does not pin its thread to a logical processor, reject samples after CPU migration, subtract an empty timing interval, collect hardware performance counters, randomize implementation order, or report confidence intervals across independent runs. The one-million-observation distributions characterize within-run behavior well, but they do not measure run-to-run uncertainty. Cache misses, branch mispredictions, translation misses, allocator activity, interrupts, and pre-emption are plausible explanations for parts of the observed tail, but they cannot be separated using the present CSV data.

Fourth, the design space is intentionally narrow. Every implementation uses vectors for FIFO storage within a price level and a hash-based order-ID index. Those shared choices may dominate cancellation at deep levels and may hide alternatives such as intrusive queues, slab allocation, stable handles, or position-aware cancellation indices. The fixed-grid designs also perform linear best-price discovery, making the comparison partly one of indexing policy as well as memory layout. This is appropriate for evaluating the implemented designs, but it leaves room for an array augmented with occupancy bitmaps, hierarchical bit sets, or cached best-price indices.

These challenges suggest several concrete directions for future work:

1. **Strengthen reproducibility and causal analysis.** Repeat every scenario across independent process runs and multiple processors; pin execution to an isolated core; control the frequency governor and background services; record processor migration; and report confidence intervals or run-level variation. Hardware counters for cache, translation, branch, allocation, and prefetch behavior would test the architectural explanations advanced in this thesis.
2. **Use trace-driven and regime-changing workloads.** Replay anonymized exchange event traces while preserving event order and timing, then segment results by instrument, volatility regime, spread, depth, and operation type. Raw or phase-labelled samples would make it possible to analyze multimodal tails and locality transitions that aggregate percentiles cannot reveal.
3. **Measure system-level performance.** Add sustained-throughput, queueing, and overload experiments alongside isolated latency. Extend the prototype with realistic risk validation, execution reporting, market-data generation, persistence, and recovery so that data-structure improvements can be evaluated as part of a complete matching path.
4. **Evaluate concurrent ownership models.** Compare instrument sharding, single-writer event loops, and carefully selected concurrent structures under multi-core load. Measurements should include cross-core communication, false sharing, NUMA placement, and the trade-off between batching for throughput and preserving tail latency.
5. **Improve fixed-grid discovery.** Add an occupancy bitmap or hierarchical summary that locates the next non-empty price without scanning the full array. This would test whether the mutation advantage of direct indexing can be retained while removing the largest weakness observed for sparse market orders.
6. **Make the hybrid boundary adaptive.** Evaluate wider and narrower windows, asymmetric bid/ask regions, volatility-dependent resizing, and recentering around a moving reference price. Any adaptive scheme must include the migration cost and preserve FIFO priority while levels move between hot and cold storage.
7. **Explore cancellation-oriented storage.** Replace linear search within a level with stable handles or position-aware indices, and compare vector removal with linked, slab-backed, or tombstone-based queues. The evaluation must account for the extra maintenance performed on insertion, matching, compaction, and index updates rather than timing cancellation alone.
8. **Validate layout optimizations end to end.** Integrate the packed record into each applicable implementation and test it under complete order-book workloads. Revisit software prefetching only for traversals that already know a useful future address, and use hardware counters to verify that hints remove misses rather than merely add instructions.

Correctness must remain a first-class constraint throughout this work. Adaptive layouts, cached best prices, stable handles, and concurrent execution all introduce additional state that can diverge from the authoritative FIFO queues. Future implementations should retain cross-design property tests, add invariant checking around representation transitions, and test recovery from persisted event sequences before performance results are considered meaningful.

## 9.3 Conclusion

This thesis investigated how memory layout and indexing strategy affect the latency of limit-order insertion, cancellation, and market-order execution in four Rust order books. Its central finding is that data layout matters through the access path of each operation. Direct price indexing is highly effective when the target price is known, ordered sparse structures are effective when the next occupied price must be discovered, field separation helps only when an operation can avoid reading unrelated data, and a hybrid representation is effective only while actual locality agrees with its configured hot region.

Fixed-tick AoS provides the strongest and most stable mutation path in several workloads and becomes the best design for long dense sweeps, but its linear search over empty prices makes it unsuitable for sparse best-price discovery. Fixed-tick SoA demonstrates that reducing the data read during an identifier search can improve some cancellation tails, yet the cost and complexity of maintaining parallel vectors limit its broader advantage. The B-tree accepts higher mutation overhead in exchange for robust ordered access across sparse and changing price ranges. Hybrid offers the most attractive balance for centrally concentrated traffic and ordinary sweeps, but its advantage is conditional: cold accesses and boundary-crossing operations can erase it.

No representation should therefore be selected from asymptotic complexity or cache-locality intuition alone. A suitable order book must be chosen against a measured workload that reflects price sparsity, queue depth, operation mix, sweep depth, and locality changes, with particular attention to tail latency. The Rust implementations and reproducible benchmarks developed for this study provide a controlled basis for making that comparison. Within the stated experimental scope, the evidence supports a broader engineering principle: the best order-book design is not the structure with the single fastest primitive, but the one whose representation matches the dominant discovery and mutation paths of its intended market workload.
